The decade, year by year

1980

hardware

Sinclair’s ZX80 undercuts everyone

Sinclair sold the ZX80 for £99.95 assembled, or £79.95 as a kit, when most computers cost several hundred pounds. It opened the British home computer market.

See the ZX Spectrum that followed

hardware

The first 5.25-inch hard disk

Shugart Technology, soon renamed Seagate, shipped the ST-506 with five megabytes of storage. Its interface set the pattern for PC hard disks for the rest of the decade.

software

dBase II puts a database on a desk

Ashton-Tate’s dBase II let a small business build and query its own records with no mainframe and no programming department behind it.

See dBase on Software

1981

hardware

The IBM PC launches

IBM announced the 5150 on 12 August, assembled from parts anyone could buy and documented in a published technical reference. Compatible machines followed within two years.

See the IBM PC 5150 on Machines

software

MS-DOS ships with the IBM PC

IBM sold it as PC DOS, but Microsoft kept the right to license MS-DOS to everyone else. That one clause shaped the industry for the next twenty years.

See MS-DOS on Software

hardware

The BBC Micro reaches British schools

Acorn won the contract for the BBC Computer Literacy Project, and the Micro became the machine a generation of schoolchildren learned to program on.

See the BBC Micro on Machines

1982

hardware

The Commodore 64 arrives

Commodore owned its own chip factory, undercut every rival, and sold through ordinary shops. The 64 became one of the best-selling computer models ever made.

See the Commodore 64 on Machines

hardware

The ZX Spectrum brings colour home

Sinclair put colour graphics into British homes for £125, and an enormous games industry grew up around the machine within two years.

See the ZX Spectrum on Machines

company

Compaq is founded

Three Texas Instruments engineers set out to build a machine that ran IBM’s software without using IBM’s code. Within two years they had the fastest-growing start-up in American history.

See the Compaq Portable on Machines

1983

software

TCP/IP becomes the standard

On 1 January the ARPANET dropped its old protocol and switched to TCP/IP. The network that became the internet now had a single common language.

software

Lotus 1-2-3 sells the IBM PC

Written in assembly language for the PC and nothing else, 1-2-3 was fast enough to feel instant. Businesses bought the hardware in order to run it.

See Lotus 1-2-3 on Software

hardware

The Apple IIe keeps the line going

Apple’s third Apple II sold in enormous numbers to schools and stayed in production for eleven years, long after the rest of the decade’s machines had gone.

See the Apple IIe on Machines

1984

hardware

The Macintosh brings the GUI home

Windows, icons, a mouse and a 3.5-inch floppy disk, in a machine aimed at people who were not engineers. It cost US$2,495 and had 128 KB of memory.

See the Macintosh on Machines

software

Tetris is written in Moscow

Alexey Pajitnov built it at a Soviet research centre on an Electronika 60, a machine with no graphics hardware, drawing the blocks out of text characters.

See Tetris on Software

company

Michael Dell starts selling from a dorm room

PC’s Limited assembled and sold PCs directly to customers, skipping the dealer entirely. The company was renamed Dell Computer four years later.

1985

hardware

The Amiga and the Atari ST arrive

Both were built on the Motorola 68000, a processor from 1979 that only now reached homes. The Amiga brought custom graphics and sound chips, the ST brought MIDI ports as standard.

See both on Machines

software

Desktop publishing begins

Aldus PageMaker, a Macintosh and Apple’s LaserWriter together moved typesetting out of the trade workshop and onto an ordinary desk.

software

Windows 1.0 ships

Microsoft’s graphical shell for MS-DOS had tiled windows that were not allowed to overlap, and it sold poorly. The line it started did not stop.

See Windows 1.0 on Software

1986

company

Compaq beats IBM to the 386

The Deskpro 386 shipped in September with Intel’s new 32-bit processor, months ahead of IBM. After this the industry stopped waiting for IBM to move first.

company

Pixar is spun out of Lucasfilm

Steve Jobs bought the computer graphics division of Lucasfilm and set it up as an independent company selling image-processing hardware.

hardware

The laptop takes shape

IBM’s PC Convertible folded shut, ran on batteries and used 3.5-inch drives. It was slow and expensive, but the shape was right.

1987

hardware

The PS/2 introduces VGA

IBM’s new line failed to win back control of the PC standard, but its VGA graphics and its keyboard and mouse ports outlived it by decades.

hardware

Acorn’s Archimedes runs on ARM

Acorn designed its own 32-bit processor rather than buy one. ARM designs now sit in most of the world’s phones.

See the BBC Micro that preceded it

software

HyperCard ships with every Mac

Bill Atkinson’s stacks of linked cards let people with no programming experience build their own software, and showed what hypertext could feel like.

1988

software

The Morris worm takes down the internet

A graduate student’s self-replicating program spread through thousands of Unix machines on 2 November. The network discovered it had a security problem.

hardware

NeXT unveils its cube

Steve Jobs’s black magnesium machine sold in small numbers. Its software is what Tim Berners-Lee used to write the first web browser two years later.

software

Tetris reaches the West

After years of tangled licensing, Tetris arrived on Western computers and then on the Game Boy, where it sold tens of millions of copies.

See Tetris on Software

1989

software

The World Wide Web is proposed

In March, Tim Berners-Lee circulated a proposal at CERN for a hypertext system linking documents across machines. His manager wrote “vague but exciting” on the cover.

software

SimCity arrives with no way to win

Maxis released a game about zoning, building and taxation that publishers had turned down for years, on the grounds that a player could not win it.

See SimCity on Software

software

Prince of Persia moves like a person

Jordan Mechner filmed his brother running and jumping and traced the frames, giving the character a weight no other platform game of the time had.

See Prince of Persia on Software
